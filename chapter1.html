READING A PHOTOGRAPH

Some of our most powerful evidence about the Gilded Age comes from photographers like Jacob Riis, who documented slum life, and Lewis Hine, who photographed child laborers - breaker boys, mill girls, tiny children at giant machines. These images changed how Americans saw their own country. But a...

What the photograph shows

What the photograph shows. Hine's pictures put a human face on statistics. A number like "two million child laborers" is abstract; a photograph of one exhausted eight-year-old is unforgettable. The images were so powerful they helped change the law.

What the photograph leaves out

What the photograph leaves out. A photographer chooses where to point the camera, whom to include, and what to leave outside the frame. Hine and Riis were reformers with a purpose - to shock the public into action. That purpose was honorable, but it means...

Your turn. Does knowing a photographer had a reform agenda make the photographs less trustworthy, or simply require you to read them carefully? PROGRESS, EXPLOITATION, OR BOTH?

Few questions divide historians of this era more than the basic one: was industrialization good or bad for America?

The case for progress

The case for progress. In a few decades, the United States went from a mostly rural nation to the greatest industrial power on earth. Industrialization produced cheaper goods, new jobs, rising overall wealth, and inventions - electric light, the telephone...

The case for exploitation

Your turn. Is it possible that both are completely true at once - that the Gilded Age was a period of real progress and real exploitation, inseparably?
