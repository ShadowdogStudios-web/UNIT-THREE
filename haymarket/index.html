The Haymarket Affair

On a rainy night in May 1886, a peaceful labor rally in Chicago’s Haymarket Square ended in gunfire and a thrown bomb. Seven police officers and at least four workers died. In the trial that followed, no one could say who threw the bomb — and that question still sits at the center of the case.

Big Question

If you can’t prove who threw the bomb, how can you justly convict anyone of it? That is the historian’s problem here.

How to read this

“Who threw the bomb?”

Haymarket is a case study in evidence. The facts of the night are murky and the sources are furious on both sides. Your job is to separate what can be proven from what people merely believed — or wanted to believe.

Evidence Trail · The cause

The eight-hour day

In the 1880s, factory workers often worked ten, twelve, even fourteen hours a day. A national movement rose around a simple demand: eight hours for work, eight for rest, eight for what we will. Chicago, the great industrial city, was its beating heart.

Core Idea

On May 1, 1886, hundreds of thousands of workers across the country struck for the eight-hour day. It is why much of the world still marks May 1 as a workers’ holiday.

Text Evidence

Two days later, at the McCormick reaper works in Chicago, police fired on strikers and killed several. Labor leaders called a protest rally for the next evening in Haymarket Square.

Why It Matters

Haymarket did not come out of nowhere. It grew from a real fight over how long a human being should have to work.

Evidence Trail · The night

The bomb

The Haymarket rally on May 4 was small and peaceful; the mayor himself watched for a while and went home. As it was ending in the rain, a column of police marched in to break it up. Someone threw a bomb into their ranks. In the chaos and gunfire that followed, people died on both sides — and to this day no one knows who threw it.

Core Idea

Seven police officers and at least four workers were killed, many of the police by other officers’ gunfire in the panic.

Text Evidence

The bomb-thrower was never identified or caught. No physical evidence tied any of the arrested men to making or throwing it.

Why It Matters

A deadly act with no known author became the basis for one of the most famous trials in American history.

Evidence Trail · The verdict

The trial

Eight men — anarchist labor organizers, most of them immigrants — were put on trial. The prosecution admitted it could not prove any of them threw the bomb. Instead it argued that their speeches and writings had inspired whoever did, and that this made them guilty of murder.

Core Idea

Seven of the eight were sentenced to death for their ideas and associations, in a city gripped by fear and a hostile press.

Text Evidence

Four were hanged in 1887; one died in his cell. Six years later, Illinois Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three survivors, declaring the trial had been unfair and the jury packed.

Why It Matters

Altgeld’s pardon cost him his career — and stands as one of the boldest acts of conscience by any American governor.

Be the Historian · Sourcing

Who threw the bomb?

This is the question the whole case turns on. Look at how far the evidence actually reaches — and where belief takes over.

What the prosecution claimed

That the anarchists’ violent speeches and newspapers had created the bomb-thrower, so the leaders were as guilty as the hand that threw it — even though that hand was never identified.

What the record shows

No witness saw any defendant throw the bomb. No physical evidence connected them to it. Several had alibis; one was not even at the square.

Be the Historian · Judgment

Crime, or idea?

The defendants were convicted. The question is what for. Weigh the two readings of the trial.

Tried for the crime

By calling for violent revolution, the argument goes, the men set the deadly night in motion and shared the blame, even if another hand threw the bomb.

Tried for the idea

With no proof of the act, the men were really punished for being radical, foreign, and frightening to a panicked city — which is why Altgeld later called the trial unjust.
